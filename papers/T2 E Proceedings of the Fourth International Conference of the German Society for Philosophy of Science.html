The articles collected in this special issue grew out of papers presented at the Fourth Inter-
national Conference of the German Society for Philosophy of Science, held at Technische
Universität Berlin from 15th until 17th August 2022. The GWP.2022 conference had origi-
nally been scheduled for 21st-23rd March 2022, but had to be rescheduled due to the so-
called ‘omicron peak’ towards the tail end of the Covid-19 pandemic. In the end, GWP.2022
was the rst major philosophy conference to be held in person again in Germany, following
the subsiding of Covid-19 over the course of 2022. Of the roughly 220 participants, about
half presented contributed papers (93 in total) or took part in one of the six symposia (each
of which featured three speakers). In total, 164 contributed papers and eight symposium
proposals had been submitted, slightly more than at the preceding GWP.2019 conference in
Cologne; this led to a somewhat more restrictive acceptance rate of just under 70%.
Accepted papers were grouped into thematic sessions, ranging from mainstream topics
in philosophy of science (such as ‘Laws of Nature’, ‘Scientic Realism’, ‘Explanation’,
and ‘Science and Values’), via issues in the philosophy of the special sciences (e.g., ‘Phi-
losophy of Physics’, ‘Organisms and Superorganisms’, ‘Psychology and Psychiatry’), to
debates intersecting with other areas in philosophy (‘Mind, Method, Mechanism’, ‘Logic
and Language’, ‘Epistemology of Science’). The titles of the six accepted symposia were
as follows: ‘Mechanisms in the Cognitive and Social Sciences’, ‘The Replication Crisis
and Philosophy of Science’, ‘The Cybernetic Renaissance’, ‘The Legitimacy of General-
izing Darwinism’, ‘Learning from Data: The Secret to Success’, and ‘Are all Laws of Phys-
ics Created Equal?’ In addition, six plenary lectures were given, including by Hans Rott
(Universität Regensburg), Mazviita Chirimuuta (University of Edinburgh), Jutta Schickore
(Indiana University), Anjan Chakravartty (University of Miami), Branden Fitelson (North-
eastern University), and Brigitte Falkenburg (Technische Universität Dortmund).
Following the conference, a call for submissions to a special issue of JGPS, based on
presentations at GWP.2022, was circulated. Owing to the fact that the conference had to be
postponed, and in light of additional organizational delays, authors were given the opportu-

The topics of the papers assembled in this special issue mirror the thematic spread of
the conference, as outlined above. Johanna Sarisoy (Edinburgh), in her paper, addresses a
core topic regarding the philosophy of experimentation, namely the eponymous question of
“Why we disagree about the success of replications”. Taking the disputed nature of vari-
ous commonly researched psychological eects (such as the ‘social priming eect’) as the
target of her study, she distinguishes between dierent epistemic goals and functions of
replications, and argues that long-standing disputes are often fuelled by lack of transparency
regarding the intended functions. Jens Harbecke (Witten/Herdecke), in his paper “Mecha-
nistic constitution as a natural law in better best systems”, builds on the existing literature
on mechanistic constitution and examines the epistemic lawlikeness of constitutive mecha-
nistic propositions. In doing so, he explores whether constitution can, in a metaphysical
sense, be considered a natural law. Christian Feldbacher-Escamilla (Düsseldorf) and Maria
Sekatskaya (Cologne) analyze the connections between “Reductionism, supervenience, and
Carnap’s account of empirical conrmability”. In doing so, they revitalize philosophical
interest in analyses that link modications of Carnap’s reductionism about the mental to
contemporary debates in the philosophy of mind. In particular, they suggest that shifting
from a metaphysical notion of supervenience to an epistemic derivative of it, opens up a
fruitful perspective on positions such as supervenience physicalism. David Hommen (Düs-
seldorf), in his contribution entitled “Poetic understanding and scientic models: models as
aesthetic descriptions” relates an issue that occupies a central place in general philosophy
of science–the question how scientic models enable representation and understanding of
real-world phenomena–to the subdiscipline of aesthetics. Building on the observation that
aesthetic descriptions often explain by way of analogy, he develops an aesthetic view of
models that can both sustain a realist stance toward science and explain why models are
epistemically valuable.
Several papers also explore issues in the philosophy of the special sciences. Thus, Paul
Näger (Münster) and Niko Strobach (Münster) contribute to an emerging branch of philoso-
phy of physics, quantum mereology. In their paper “A taxonomy for the mereology of entan-
gled quantum systems”, they develop an exhaustive taxonomy of six distinct and precise
models of entangled quantum systems, which aims to provide information both about the
entangled property and about the mereological features of the system. Vito Balorda (Rijeka)
and Predrag Šustar (Rijeka), in their paper “Natural selection: a mechanism, a pathway, or
a little bit of both?” contribute to the philosophy of biology by examining the question of
how best to characterize natural selection. Whereas standard accounts view natural selection
variously as a force, a statistical concept, or a causal structure (among others), Balorda and
Šustar liken the way natural selection operates to that of pathways (where ‘pathway’ here
refers to a sequence of causal steps that links an upstream cause via some causal intermedi-
ates to a downstream eect).
Other papers explicitly pursue links between the philosophy of science and neighbouring
elds such as epistemology and philosophy of mind. Thus, Christoph Merdes (Kraków), in
his paper “Dishonest testimony and the virtue of testimonial justice: a naive Bayesian analy-
sis”, adopts tools from formal epistemology in order to develop a classication of dishonest

agents and, using agent-based modeling, develops insights into whether testimonial justice
may serve as a safeguard against such agents in various epistemic contexts. Matej Kohár
(Berlin) draws on the philosophy of cognition in his discussion of the ‘scaling-up problem’,
viz. the challenges that arise when attempting to account, in non-representationalist terms,
for cases where a cognitive system is sensitive to absent objects, abstract properties, or other
situations in which direct interaction with the environment does not suce to account for
the system’s behaviour. Attempting to address “The scaling-up problem from a mechanistic

ity’, one can identify an important heuristic for representation-hungry phenomena, thereby
resolving the epistemic dimension of the scaling-up problem.
Finally, reecting the diversity of systematic approaches and historical perspectives tra-
ditionally included in the GWP conferences, a number of papers address questions from the
history of philosophy of science. Filip Buyse (Antwerp/Pisa), in his paper “The physiologist
Johannes Peter Müller and the philosopher Spinoza: an underestimated relation”, traces how
the sensory physiology developed by Müller in the second quarter of the nineteenth century
was inspired by, and reects insights gained from, Müller’s reception of Spinoza’s Ethics.
Matthew Lund (Glassboro), in his paper “Bessel and the epistemology of observational
relativity”, discusses how Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel’s discovery, in the 1830s, of ‘invol-
untary constant dierences’ in observed astronomical transit times by dierent observers,
spurred psychological investigations into the processes of visual perception. Sebastian Sun-
day Grève (Beijing), in his paper “Turing’s philosophy of AI”, oers a reconstruction of the
history of Alan Turing’s work on issues that would nowadays broadly fall under the rubric
of ‘articial intelligence’. In particular, the paper analyzes how Turing uses terms such as
‘intelligence’, ‘computer’, and ‘learning’ as conceptual devices with the aim of fostering
clear and rigorous discourse, rather than as stipulative denitions.
Taken together, the eleven papers published in this special issue convey a sense of the
breadth, depth, and variety of topics discussed at GWP.2022; as such they are testament
to the lively and dynamic character of contemporary philosophy of science, in Germany,
Europe, and beyond. The guest editors–Axel Gelfert, Birgit Beck, and Beate Krickel–
would like to express their gratitude towards both the external reviewers for their diligence
and time, as well as to the authors for their patience during what, at times, was a somewhat
protracted editorial process.
